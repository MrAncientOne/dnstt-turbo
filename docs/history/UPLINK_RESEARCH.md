## Recent primary research

The following papers study actual BBR implementations or wireless testbeds, rather than asserting that adding a bandwidth estimator guarantees better performance. They are not evaluations of this project's nested KCP/DNS transport.

1. **Gomez, Kfoury, Crichigno and Srivastava, “Evaluating TCP BBRv3 performance in wired broadband networks.”** Peer-reviewed journal article, *Computer Communications* 222, 198–208; online **1 May 2024**, issue **1 June 2024**, DOI `10.1016/j.comcom.2024.04.037`. Their emulated and hardware experiments vary delay, loss, buffers and flow counts. They find good loss resilience around 1% loss and low queue occupancy, but coexistence depends on buffer size and flow population; FQ-CoDel mitigates RTT unfairness. **Testing implication:** compare several queue sizes in BDP units and unequal-RTT competing flows, not only isolated bulk speed. **Limit:** these are selected wired topologies and the tested 2024 implementation, not all wireless paths or the latest draft. [Author-hosted published paper](https://gomezgaona.github.io/online-cv/assets/pdfs/bbr3_comcom.pdf), [publisher metadata](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0140366424001658).

2. **Zeynali, Weyulu, Fathalli, Chandrasekaran and Feldmann, “Promises and Potential of BBRv3.”** Peer-reviewed conference paper, **PAM, 11–13 March 2024**, LNCS 14538, 249–272, DOI `10.1007/978-3-031-56252-5_12`. Across tested buffer sizes, RTTs, loss and flow-size distributions, the authors report persistent fairness concerns. In one large-buffer case, a BBRv3 flow joining 15 seconds later took over four minutes to reach equitable sharing. **Testing implication:** include staggered starts and longer convergence runs; a short simultaneous-start test can conceal a problem. **Limit:** these findings concern the examined early BBRv3 revision and configurations; they do not establish current-version behavior everywhere. [Authors' experiment site and paper](https://inet-bbrv3eval.mpi-inf.mpg.de/), [institutional publication record](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/promises-andpotential-ofbbrv3/).

3. **The same authors, “BBRv3 in the public Internet: a boon or a bane?”** Peer-reviewed **ANRW workshop paper, 23 July 2024**, 97–99, DOI `10.1145/3673422.3674889`. In their 100 Mbps, 100 ms dumbbell tests with specific ECN/AQM settings, one BBRv3 flow could obtain over 99% of the capacity while five CUBIC flows competed. **Testing implication:** measure background-flow starvation and test ECN/AQM configurations explicitly when using real kernel TCP. **Limit:** the title is broader than the experiment: this is a controlled testbed result, not a measurement of every public Internet path; BBRv3 code and ECN configuration matter. [Author-hosted paper](https://balakrishnanc.github.io/papers/zeynali-anrw2024.pdf), [peer-review/publication record](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/bbrv3-in-the-public-internet-a-boon-or-a-bane/).

4. **Shrestha, Pokhrel and Kua, “TCP BBR Performance over Wi-Fi 6: AQM Impacts and Cross-Layer Insights.”** **Preprint**, arXiv `2512.18259`; first submitted **20 December 2025**, checked version **v2, 29 March 2026**. No journal/conference publication is identified on the checked arXiv record, so peer review is not assumed. Their fully wireless testbed compares FIFO, FQ-CoDel and CAKE during upload, download and bidirectional traffic. They report better latency/fairness with AQM, while CAKE can still produce brief retransmission bursts during bandwidth probing. **Testing implication:** combine variable radio service with queue draining and bursty feedback; “CAKE enabled” is not itself a correctness/performance proof. **Limit:** testbed-specific observations, not universal numerical gains or a recommendation to install fixed-rate shaping on every tunnel. [Versioned preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.18259v2), [full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.18259v2).

These results disagree in some coexistence outcomes because versions, queue policies, delay, population and evaluation durations differ. Save all those parameters with benchmark results. Do not transfer a paper's numerical gain to this tunnel without a matched experiment.

## What this means for the controller

The BBRv3 draft distinguishes application-limited delivery from a saturated path. Low application-limited samples should not reduce the bandwidth maximum; a higher application-limited sample can raise it. It measures delivery across both sending and acknowledgment intervals to resist ACK compression. It also models loss and inflight limits, rather than relying only on a peak bandwidth estimate and minimum RTT. Its appendix includes startup, ACK aggregation, application-limited operation, probing and idle-restart cases. **Inference:** the project's handshake/idle protection is necessary, but does not make BBR-lite equivalent to BBRv3; targeted tests must cover compressed feedback and recovery as well as bandwidth plateaus. [BBRv3 draft, sections 4.1, 5.5.4 and Appendix A](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/draft-ietf-ccwg-bbr-06).

QUIC's standard separates minimum RTT from smoothed RTT and variance, specifies exponentially backed-off probes, and requires pacing or bounded bursts. ACK-only packets should avoid pacing delay. **Inference:** reserve progress for ACK/control traffic under simultaneous upload and download; measure burst size and recovery delay, not just mean transfer speed. Borrowing these principles does not justify copying QUIC timer constants into a TCP-carried KCP session. [RFC 9002, sections 5–7](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9002.html).

## Model the actual carrier

This project's protocol scheduling can also limit throughput before the network does. An RTT-derived flight window may be too small when the protocol only flushes queued data on a longer interval. **Engineering inference from the project's controlled tests:** account for that service interval when provisioning flight headroom, while keeping explicit byte/memory bounds and checking loaded latency. Increasing headroom is not implementing BBRv3, and should be tested against slow links and abrupt capacity reductions. Similarly, a brief interval with active demand but no completed delivery need not establish a zero-capacity path; keep short feedback gaps distinct from a sustained outage, and preserve bounded outage backoff. These are project findings/recommendations, not results claimed by the papers above.

TCP exposes an ordered reliable byte stream and repairs IP losses by retransmission. A lost outer TCP segment therefore usually appears to DNS/KCP as a pause followed by ordered delivery, not as a permanently missing DNS frame. **Inference:** the main TCP-carrier simulator should preserve DNS frame order and impose head-of-line stalls, feedback batching and finite write buffers. Separate frame-dropping tests remain useful for UDP, proxy failure or application-level record loss, but must be labeled accordingly. [RFC 9293, section 2.2](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9293.html#section-2.2).

One ordered TCP carrier shared by many multiplexed streams can delay all streams behind missing outer bytes. More KCP window or application buffering cannot eliminate that dependency. QUIC explicitly avoids head-of-line blocking across independent streams. **Inference:** assess small-request latency during bulk transfers and outer stalls; do not promise that this transport can match a UDP VPN under every loss pattern. [RFC 9000, section 13](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9000.html#section-13).

DNS-over-TCP requires unique IDs for outstanding queries, response matching even when replies are prepared out of order, pipelining and connection reuse. Readers must tolerate partial length prefixes and messages. **Inference:** test exhausted ID space, delayed old responses, fragmented/coalesced reads and reconnects while queries remain pending. [RFC 7766, sections 6–8](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7766.html).

Asymmetric paths need particular attention to reverse traffic and bidirectional contention. Saturating the slow direction can delay feedback needed to fill the fast direction. **Inference:** test slow-upload/fast-download and the reverse, with simultaneous bulk and sparse traffic; count control bytes separately from payload. [RFC 3449, sections 3 and 5](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc3449.html).

## Recommended test matrix

These parameter values are project recommendations, not thresholds prescribed by the cited standards.

| Dimension | Cases to exercise | Evidence to record |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Capacity and asymmetry | 64 kbps, 256 kbps, 1, 10, 100, 500 and 1,000+ Mbps; ratios 1:1, 1:10 and 1:100 | Payload goodput, actual wire rate, overhead, CPU and memory; identify process limits separately from link limits. |
| Delay and jitter | RTT 20, 100, 200 and 1,000 ms; correlated jitter and occasional long tails | Small-stream p50/p95/max completion time; bulk progress and bandwidth-estimator error. |
| Capacity transitions | Step down 10× and back up; repeating fluctuating capacity; changing propagation delay | Time to drain excessive queues and regain throughput; no long-lived collapse. |
| Carrier disruptions | Ordered stalls in either direction; 3–10 second blackout; ACK batching; disconnect/reconnect | Integrity, bounded queues, feedback backoff and time to useful progress after restoration. |
| Applications | Upload/download alone and together; 1, 4 and 16 streams; tiny requests during bulk; idle then bulk; slow receiver | No byte mismatch or unexplained stream reset; latency and fairness under load. |
| Queue environments | Small and large finite queues, tail drop and AQM, competing background traffic | Queue delay, drops/retransmissions and starvation; kernel TCP tests with CUBIC/BBR when available. |

Wireless evaluation should include abrupt capacity changes, unrelated loss and load from other users; a quiet fixed-rate link misses the relevant dynamics. Warm-up and measurement periods should be distinct. [RFC 8869, sections 2–3](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8869.html). For live comparisons, alternate baseline and tunnel runs using identical endpoints/workloads and record both adjacent baselines; repeat runs instead of comparing tests made during different uplink conditions. This is a project measurement recommendation.

## Linux emulation and shaping

The upstream netem manual supports seeded random and burst loss, correlated delay, reordering, rate and slotting. Slotting approximates bursty Wi-Fi/LTE scheduling. It warns that kernel timer resolution can distort rates into bursts and that realistic TCP netem tests require receiver ingress placement because of TCP Small Queues. **Inference:** use isolated namespaces/IFB when available, independent directions and saved seeds; validate the emulated baseline before trusting results. In-memory ordered tests are reproducible protocol tests, not substitutes for Linux TCP emulation. [Upstream iproute2 netem manual](https://github.com/iproute2/iproute2/blob/main/man/man8/tc-netem.8).

CAKE combines shaping, AQM and flow isolation. Its arrival-rate estimator cannot infer bandwidth downstream of itself. An explicit bandwidth setting constrains throughput. **Inference:** a fixed inner-tunnel shaper can cap a recovered faster uplink, while observed low demand does not establish low capacity. Compare shaping off/on; document where measurements occur and whether they include encapsulation. Host fairness inside the tunnel and congestion control on its outer carrier address different queues. [Upstream iproute2 CAKE manual](https://github.com/iproute2/iproute2/blob/main/man/man8/tc-cake.8).

## Acceptance and limits

Require byte integrity, completion or explicit bounded failure, cancellation/shutdown progress and bounded resource use. Keep throughput/latency results visible even when integrity passes. Define comparative performance thresholds for each workload and retain failing seeds. These are project acceptance recommendations.

BCP 133 calls for evaluation of congestion collapse, queueing, loss, short flows and coexistence, including tunnels and changing wireless paths. A high bulk speed alone is insufficient evidence of a good controller. No finite simulated matrix proves maximum throughput for every uplink; real kernel transport tests and repeated field measurements remain necessary. [RFC 9743](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9743.html).
